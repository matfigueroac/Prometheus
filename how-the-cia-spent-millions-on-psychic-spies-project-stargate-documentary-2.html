How The CIA Spent Millions On Psychic Spies - PROJECT: STARGATE (Documentary 2 of 3)

Summary

The second part is the most important if you want to understand how the documentary tries to defend the seriousness of Stargate. It partially corrects the excess of part 1 by questioning Ed Dames's method, looks for Joseph McMoneagle and Edwin May, introduces metrics, agencies, tasking and operational examples, and then re-complicates the picture with the Uri Geller block. It is the episode where the series comes closest to an institutional argument, although it continues to depend heavily on testimony and storytelling.

What does it actually contain?

  • Explicit criticism of the departure of Ed Dames from the double-blind protocol
  • long interview with Joseph McMoneagle about GONDOLA WISH, recruitment and first tests
  • Edwin May's explanation of subject selection, longitudinal data, and training limits
  • Quantitative claims about accuracy, missions and agency returns
  • examples of operational tracking and location
  • controversial block on Uri Geller
  • cliffhanger that pushes towards Mars 1,000,000 BC

Why it matters within X Files

  • is the best secondary piece of the package to map the pro-Stargate argument from insiders
  • connect directly with ZZZXPH0XZZZ
  • installs figures, agencies and concrete stories that are then recycled in articles, podcasts and online lore
  • allows you to distinguish between serious insider testimony and scaletion toward weaker claims

Episode structure

1) Methodological rectification after Ed Dames

The episode opens with a valuable distance shot: the host says that the protocol taught by Dames does not match the original SRI standard because Dames knows the target. This objection is key because it finally introduces the central methodological problem.

Core sentence:

  • he doesn't use the double-blind system ... meaning Dames knows what the target is

2) McMoneagle and the operational origin

The episode travels to Virginia to speak with Joseph McMoneagle and present his institutional pedigree. Here the documentary stops relying only on demonstrations and moves on to archives, documents and a career story.

Claims/relevant contents:

  • McMoneagle introduces himself as the first one recruited
  • GONDOLA WISH appears as early name of the effort
  • memo on Soviet threat and paranormal use cited in context OPSEC
  • It is insisted that the initial motivation was to determine if the phenomenon represented a real threat

3) Edwin May and the pseudo-scientific/experimental layer

Edwin May provides the strongest segment of the episode because it talks about population selection, laboratory and time series.

Important points:

  • It is stated that some 600 people from selected populations were checked
  • the estimate offered is that around 1% could meet laboratory standards
  • May maintains that training does not improve indefinitely: people can climb up to their native ceiling, not much more
  • describes decades of data on McMoneagle and other viewers

This hardens the piece because it shifts the discussion from pure testimony to something that at least pretends to be an accumulated corpus.

4) Metrics, tasking and institutional return

Here appears the heart of the episode: if 17 of 19 agencies returned with new missions, then the program generated value.

Strong claims of the documentary:

  • 85 percent of the information collected from remote viewing was accurate
  • 50 percent of 700 missions produced usable information
  • 504 separate missions
  • 19 federal agencies
  • 17 returned with new additional missions

These figures are narratively powerful because they condense success, scale, and persistence. Even so, in the piece itself they are not audited with sufficient granularity.

5) Tracking and localization cases

McMoneagle relates several examples of operational monitoring: wind/generators, Stanford accelerator, Los Alamos, microwave equipment, maritime smuggling. The pattern is always the same:

  • a minimum target is received
  • drawing/description occurs
  • the client says the hit was impressive

This block matters more as internal operational folklore than as closed proof.

6) Uri Geller

The episode introduces Uri Geller and with that shifts the tone from insider history to a more slippery pop-esoteric zone. Geller's mere presence makes the chapter more viral, but less robust.

Characters and positions

  • Joseph McMoneagle: main vector of testimonial credibility
  • Edwin May: main vector of technical/experimental credibility
  • Ed Dames: negative methodological contrast at the beginning
  • Uri Geller: figure of media amplification and controversy
  • host of the documentary: he plays a moderate prosecutor, but not a tough skeptic

Dialogue / core claims

> Selection of fragments and argument nuclei from the local transcript.

Methodological objection

  • he doesn't use the double-blind system during his protocol
  • this seems highly convenient to me

GONDOLA WISH and Soviet threat

  • I was the first one recruited for the Target program which back then was called Gondola Wish
  • The Soviets have the ability to collect intelligence using paranormal means and are believed to be doing so

Selection and training

  • roughly 600 people
  • about one percent of the selective populations could meet laboratory standards
  • you can learn and improve up to whatever your native skill level happens to be

Claimed effectiveness metrics

  • 85 percent of the information collected from remote viewing was accurate
  • 50 percent of 700 missions produced usable information
  • 504 separate missions
  • 19 federal agencies
  • 17 of them returned with new additional missions

Examples of perceived validation

  • it worked and it worked really well
  • some of the stuff they were getting was stuff they couldn't get any other way
  • they loved us we were damn good at that job

What does this episode really contribute?

  • gives concrete context to ZZZXPH0XZZZ
  • presents McMoneagle and May as first-order sources within Stargate lore
  • leaves better methodological questions than part 1
  • helps map what numbers and anecdotes support the pro-Stargate case

What doesn't solve

  • does not show a complete audit of the figures cited
  • does not allow you to review how accurate or usable information was defined
  • mixes too fast laboratory, tasking, briefings, operations and television
  • the inclusion of Uri Geller weakens the critical discipline of the chapter

Critical reading

The most solid

  • initial objection to Dames protocol
  • presence of McMoneagle and May
  • anchoring to declassified documents and institutional language

The weakest

  • reliance on quantitative claims without full on-screen traceability
  • operational examples that are difficult to audit by the viewer
  • Uri Geller block as a mixture of showmanship and media parapsychology

Useful interpretation

If part 1 is onboarding, part 2 is defense briefing. It is advisable to read it not as definitive proof, but as a compendium of how insiders retrospectively justify that the program had real value.
