Uncanny / Liminal Research Map

Summary

This is the master category note for the Uncanny / Liminal lane inside x-files. It connects anthropology, psychology, architecture, nostalgia, internet aesthetics, analog horror, haunted media and weird fiction into one reusable framework.

The core idea: many modern “weird” phenomena do not feel strange because they are completely alien. They feel strange because they are almost familiar: a school hallway without students, a house with impossible geometry, a mall after closing, a childhood TV signal, an empty hotel corridor, a yellow office maze, a fake documentary archive, a room that remembers people but no longer contains them.

What it actually contains

  • definitions of liminality, the uncanny, non-places, hauntology and liminal-space aesthetics
  • a classification system for future X Files notes
  • a map of why these themes produce fear, nostalgia, melancholy and mysticism
  • relationships with backrooms-liminal-spaces-deep-dive, backrooms-kane-pixels-timeline and house-of-leaves-labyrinth-uncanny-architecture
  • a critical reading rule so this lane does not become only “vibes”

Why it matters inside X Files

The wiki already has UFO/UAP, CIA/Stargate, Mars anomalies and ancient civilizations as evidence/lore packages. Uncanny / Liminal adds a different axis: not anomalous objects in the sky or lost civilizations, but anomalous experience.

This lane is useful because it explains why some ordinary things feel like secret evidence of another layer of reality:

  • architecture that seems to contain more than it should
  • everyday spaces that become dreamlike when emptied
  • media formats that look like recovered files
  • childhood aesthetics that return as ghost signals
  • fictional documents that imitate real archives
  • internet communities turning atmosphere into mythology

In short: this category is the bridge between psychology, haunted architecture, internet folklore and weird fiction.

Core vocabulary

Liminality

Liminality means threshold-state. In anthropology, Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner used it for the middle phase of rites of passage: the subject is no longer what they were, but not yet what they will become.

Useful X Files translation:

  • between before and after
  • between identity and non-identity
  • between use and abandonment
  • between reality and fiction
  • between ordinary space and symbolic space

A liminal object or place is not merely empty. It is suspended.

Liminal space aesthetic

A liminal space image usually shows a place of transit, waiting or temporary use — corridor, mall, airport, pool, hotel, school, office, parking lot — stripped of the people and functions that normally explain it.

The effect is produced by:

  • absence of expected life
  • transitional architecture
  • repetitive geometry
  • low-resolution or dated visual texture
  • unclear time period
  • weak local identity
  • a sense that the viewer arrived either too late or too early

The internet version became highly visible around 2019–2020, especially through Backrooms imagery, Reddit/TikTok curation and pandemic-era empty public spaces.

The uncanny

The uncanny is not the same as the unknown. It is the strangely familiar.

Useful definition: the uncanny appears when something normally safe, homely or intelligible returns in a distorted form. Freud’s unheimlich literally points toward the “un-homely”: what should feel like home becomes not-home.

X Files applications:

  • the home that becomes a labyrinth
  • the doll, automaton or entity that is almost alive
  • the familiar room that feels staged by something else
  • the repeated image that looks like memory but behaves like evidence
  • the archive that seems official but cannot be verified

Non-place

Marc Augé’s concept of the non-place helps explain anonymous infrastructures: airports, hotels, malls, highways, gas stations, waiting rooms. They are built for circulation rather than belonging.

In X Files terms, non-places become powerful when their normal flow disappears. The place remains, but the social contract that made it legible is gone.

Hauntology

Hauntology is the feeling that culture is haunted by futures that never arrived and pasts that refuse to disappear. In music and internet aesthetics, it often appears as degraded media, old educational films, VHS ghosts, dead malls, obsolete technology and nostalgic textures.

It matters here because liminal content often feels like a memory of a future/past that cannot be located.

Emotional mechanics

1. Familiarity without safety

The viewer recognizes the format: school, office, house, pool, mall. But the normal signs of safety are missing. That contradiction creates unease.

2. Absence as presence

The place feels inhabited by absence. There is no person, but there are traces of people: chairs, carpets, lights, toys, signs, doors, stains, hallways.

This is why liminal images can feel haunted without ghosts.

3. Nostalgia as contamination

Nostalgia is central. The image often resembles childhood, old video games, early internet, suburban spaces, 1990s/2000s interiors or half-remembered family trips. But the comfort is contaminated by stillness and derealization.

Formula:

childhood memory + empty architecture + low-resolution texture + missing human context = liminal nostalgia

4. Spatial derealization

Repetition, scale ambiguity and lack of human reference make the viewer question whether the place is real, dreamlike, simulated or endless.

Backrooms literalizes this: everyday architecture becomes infinite.

5. Archive effect

Many uncanny/liminal stories are presented as documents: tapes, files, photographs, footnotes, reports, incident logs, missing-person traces. The pseudo-documentary form makes fiction feel discoverable rather than invented.

This links directly to house-of-leaves-labyrinth-uncanny-architecture, Kane Pixels, SCP-style fiction and CIA/Stargate document culture.

Secret / mystical layer

The mystical appeal is not necessarily supernatural in a doctrinal sense. It is the intuition that ordinary reality has thresholds, seams or backstage zones.

Recurring mystical assumptions:

  • buildings can hide impossible interiors
  • reality has maintenance spaces
  • dreams and memory may preserve real places
  • old media can transmit dead signals
  • childhood spaces are emotionally charged portals
  • an image can be evidence of a place that should not exist
  • repetition can reveal a hidden structure beneath the ordinary

This explains why liminal spaces connect so well with:

  • Backrooms and noclip mythology
  • analog horror
  • cursed images
  • found footage
  • haunted house fiction
  • occult readings of architecture
  • simulation/backstage-of-reality theories

Relation to nostalgia

Nostalgia here is not only longing. It is often nostalgia without object: the viewer misses a place they may never have visited.

Useful subtypes:

  • childhood-place nostalgia — schools, pools, arcades, playrooms, carpeted halls
  • retail nostalgia — dead malls, department stores, food courts, neon signage
  • media nostalgia — VHS, CRT, public-access TV, old games, educational tapes
  • suburban nostalgia — basements, model homes, empty streets, hotel corridors
  • institutional nostalgia — hospitals, offices, government training rooms, conference centers

The emotional charge comes from a contradiction: the image feels intimate but impersonal.

Relation to architecture

Uncanny/liminal architecture tends to use:

  • corridors
  • thresholds
  • stairs
  • doors that imply more rooms
  • identical rooms
  • fluorescent light
  • synthetic carpet
  • reflective tiles
  • windows that do not orient the viewer
  • impossible scale
  • lack of exterior reference

The architectural secret is that these are usually support spaces, not symbolic spaces. Once isolated, they become symbolic.

Relation to media and internet culture

The internet did not invent liminality, but it standardized it as an aesthetic category.

Key mechanisms:

  • anonymous image boards and repost culture remove context
  • low-resolution compression intensifies uncertainty
  • collective tagging turns atmosphere into taxonomy
  • creepypasta converts image into mythology
  • analog horror adds institutional evidence and timelines
  • TikTok/Reddit/YouTube accelerate recognition and imitation

This makes the category a hybrid of theory and folk taxonomy.

Classification system for future notes

Use this taxonomy when adding new material:

A. Liminal architecture

Dominant question: what spatial pattern creates threshold emotion?

Examples:

  • empty malls
  • schools after hours
  • hotel corridors
  • airports at night
  • parking structures
  • pools, locker rooms, basements

B. Uncanny domesticity

Dominant question: how does home become not-home?

Examples:

  • haunted houses
  • impossible rooms
  • duplicated family spaces
  • dolls, mannequins, almost-human figures
  • suburban horror

C. Haunted media / hauntology

Dominant question: what obsolete medium returns like a ghost?

Examples:

  • VHS horror
  • old TV broadcasts
  • degraded game footage
  • lost media myths
  • educational tapes
  • dead websites

D. Pseudo-archive / found document

Dominant question: how does the format simulate evidence?

Examples:

  • reports
  • footnotes
  • logs
  • incident files
  • classified memos
  • fake scholarly apparatus

E. Infinite interior / impossible geometry

Dominant question: what spatial impossibility breaks reality?

Examples:

  • House of Leaves
  • Backrooms
  • endless corridors
  • non-Euclidean rooms
  • buildings bigger inside than outside

F. Nostalgic anomaly

Dominant question: why does the object feel remembered before it is known?

Examples:

  • dreamcore
  • weirdcore
  • childhood rooms
  • abandoned playgrounds
  • retro corporate interiors

Reading rule

A note belongs in this category if at least two of these are true:

  • the ordinary becomes strange
  • the space/media is transitional or abandoned
  • nostalgia and dread coexist
  • the format imitates evidence or recovered media
  • the place feels larger, older or more secret than it should
  • absence behaves like a presence
  • the viewer/reader must navigate uncertainty rather than receive a simple story

Critical reading

Strong claims

  • Liminality is a serious anthropological term, though internet usage is broader and looser.
  • The uncanny is a durable psychological/literary concept for familiar strangeness.
  • Liminal-space aesthetics are a documented internet phenomenon with recognizable visual conventions.
  • Backrooms and House of Leaves are strong anchor texts because both convert architecture into existential horror.

Weak / speculative claims

  • “This image is paranormal” is usually unsupported.
  • Not every empty place is liminal in a meaningful sense.
  • Nostalgia is subjective and culturally patterned.
  • The mystical reading is valuable as interpretation, not evidence.

Useful stance

Treat uncanny/liminal material as experiential evidence, not empirical evidence. It tells us how people perceive thresholds, memory, architecture and media; it does not prove that the threshold is literally supernatural.
